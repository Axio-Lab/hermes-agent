# Writing editor

Use this reference when editing a draft or detecting AI-writing patterns.

## Editing principles

- Preserve the writer's point, vocabulary, cadence, humor, uncertainty,
  bluntness, digressions, and level of polish.
- Make the minimum effective edit. Fix slop, errors, repetition, and unclear
  passages. Leave strong human sentences alone.
- Lead with the point when setup adds nothing. Keep setup that adds context,
  tension, character, or a necessary story.
- Front-load information only when it improves clarity. Do not force every
  paragraph into the same point-detail-background shape.
- Keep the user's meaning. Ask rather than inventing claims, examples,
  statistics, quotes, sources, or opinions.
- Open difficult writing up without dumbing it down. Replace tangled syntax,
  jargon, and empty abstraction while protecting nuance and precision.
- Prefer active voice and named actors. Do not force active voice when the
  actor is unknown or genuinely irrelevant.
- Keep uncertainty when it is honest. Phrases such as "I think" and "maybe"
  are not filler when they accurately qualify the claim or belong to the
  writer's voice.
- Use concrete names, dates, numbers, mechanisms, and examples already
  supported by the draft. Never replace a useful detail with generic
  importance.
- Let direct verbs carry the sentence. Prefer "decided" to "made a decision"
  and "can" to "has the ability to."
- Preserve useful edge, opinions, profanity, self-interruptions, and odd but
  clear details. Do not make the writer safer or more professional by default.
- Keep the existing structure unless it hurts the piece. Explain meaningful
  reorganization in the final note.

## Words and phrases to challenge

Cut inflated words when they add no precise meaning: delve, foster, leverage,
utilize, facilitate, empower, streamline, robust, cutting-edge, paradigm
shift, game changer, tapestry, realm, beacon, multifaceted, meticulous,
intricate, paramount, transformative, elevate, embark, supercharge, harness,
ever-evolving.

Challenge often-empty adverbs: just, literally, honestly, simply, actually,
truly, fundamentally, importantly, crucially, inherently, inevitably. Keep
them when they carry real emphasis, contrast, uncertainty, or spoken rhythm.

Cut delayed-point phrases unless they are genuinely part of the writer's
voice: "it's worth noting," "at the end of the day," "when it comes to," "at
its core," "in today's world," "the reality is," "in terms of," "with regard
to," "in order to," "going forward," "in this article," and "let's dive in."

## Named patterns

### Binary contrast

Pattern: "This is not X. It is Y," "The question is not X, it is Y," or "It is
not just X but Y."

Fix: State Y directly unless the contrast carries essential meaning.

### Throat-clearing opener

Pattern: "Here's the thing," "Let me be clear," "I'll be honest," or "The
uncomfortable truth is."

Fix: Remove the announcement and make the claim.

### Faux-insight setup

Pattern: "What nobody tells you," "What most people get wrong," or "The part
everyone misses."

Fix: Cut the self-flattering setup and test whether the claim stands alone.

### Colon reveal

Pattern: A dramatic setup followed by a colon and a lowercase reveal, such as
"The best part: it learns."

Fix: Write a normal sentence. Keep colons for lists, labels, explanations, and
quotations.

### Superficial analysis

Pattern: A trailing `-ing` clause with words such as "highlighting,"
"underscoring," "reflecting," or "showcasing" that claims significance without
explaining a mechanism.

Fix: State the consequence, evidence, or mechanism. Delete the clause if none
exists.

### Importance puffery

Pattern: "Stands as a testament," "marks a pivotal moment," "plays a vital
role," "solidifies its position," or "underscores its significance."

Fix: State the fact and let the reader judge its importance.

### Weasel attribution

Pattern: "Experts agree," "industry reports suggest," "many argue," "studies
show," or "widely regarded as."

Fix: Name a real source already available in the draft. Otherwise flag the
claim or ask for support.

### Fake-strong verb

Pattern: "Serves as," "stands as," "boasts," or another inflated substitute
for "is," "has," or a concrete action.

Fix: Use the plain verb or describe what the subject actually does.

### Synonym cycling

Pattern: Repeatedly renaming the same thing to avoid using the clear noun.

Fix: Repeat the accurate term. Consistency is clearer than decorative variety.

### Negative listing

Pattern: "Not an X. Not a Y. A Z."

Fix: Say Z.

### Dramatic fragmentation

Pattern: Stacked fragments such as "That's it. That's the whole thing." or
"X. And Y. And Z."

Fix: Use a complete sentence unless the fragment genuinely matches the
writer's established cadence.

### Robotic rhythm

Pattern: Repeated sentence lengths, identical paragraph shapes, repeated
three-part lists, or a stack of polished punch lines.

Fix: Vary structure only where the thought requires it. Do not add randomness
for its own sake.

### Rhetorical setup

Pattern: "What if I told you," "Think about it," "Plot twist," or a
self-answered question used as stage machinery.

Fix: Make the point directly.

### Fake-profound kicker

Pattern: A final metaphor, aphorism, or mic-drop line that inflates a finished
argument.

Fix: Delete it. End on the strongest concrete point, takeaway, or next action.

### Summary-recap ending

Pattern: "In conclusion," "Ultimately," "Overall," or a final paragraph that
only repeats what the reader just read.

Fix: End on a concrete implication or action.

### Formatting slop

Pattern: Emoji headings, decorative bold, bullets that should be prose,
headings over tiny sections, or title case applied mechanically.

Fix: Use structure only when the content needs it.

## Edit workflow

1. Read the complete draft before changing it.
2. Identify the core point and three to five voice signals to preserve.
3. Make the minimum effective edit.
4. Check the result against `writing-eval.md`.
5. Fix every failed check and read the draft aloud in your head once more.
6. Return the full edited draft and a short `What changed` section.

## Detect workflow

For each pattern found:

1. Name the pattern from this reference.
2. Quote the smallest excerpt that proves it.
3. Give a short suggested fix.

Do not rewrite the draft, assign an AI probability, score it, or claim to know
who or what wrote it. Offer an edit after the findings.
